# Tether-Cinched Overhead Flotation Hoop with Force-Limited Release for Ferry Passengers

### Why We Invented This Technology
Ferry and small passenger-vessel riders (roughly 2+ billion ferry passenger trips a year worldwide, plus cruise and excursion passengers) face a quiet but real friction: in an emergency, or even a routine drill, they must find a lifejacket stowed under a seat or in an overhead locker, work out an unfamiliar tangle of straps, buckles and a crotch strap, and fit it correctly, which commonly takes untrained adults 1-2 minutes and often ends with the jacket donned wrongly or not at all. For children, elderly and disabled passengers it is worse, since sizes are mixed and stowage is often out of reach. The depth is low on an ordinary day (a few seconds of ignored safety briefing) but severe when it matters: in capsizes of overloaded or fast-sinking ferries, which kill hundreds to thousands of people per year, mostly in South and Southeast Asia and Africa, jackets that cannot be found and donned in under 30 seconds are effectively absent. I did not run a search, so these figures are order-of-magnitude estimates from memory rather than verified statistics.

### The Innovation
## Abstract
A foam-fronted flotation hoop hangs open in plain sight above each ferry seat, like a transit strap-hanger. The passenger grips two handles and pulls it down over the head, so it lands under the armpits with the arms outside. A slack lanyard joins the hoop's cinch tail to the vessel. As the wearer moves away, the lanyard draws the tail through a one-way buckle and shrinks the hoop until band tension reaches a preset value. A calibrated breakaway coupling then releases. Fit is therefore set by force, not length, so one device fits girths of 70–140 cm with no search, sizing, buckling or instruction. The 5–10 s donning time is an untested estimate.

## Background & Prior Work
Passenger lifejackets are stowed in lockers or under seats and need several ordered actions: find, unpack, orient, don, buckle, tighten. The LSA Code only requires that 75% of unfamiliar persons don correctly within one minute without help (MSC.207(81)), which is longer than a fast capsize allows.

The closest prior art found:
- **Belt-pack inflatables** (US 5,702,279; US 6,394,866): placed over the head and tightened by a hand-pulled drawstrap. They are worn or stowed and manually sized.
- **Helicopter rescue strop** (Lifesaving Systems catalog): passes over the head and sits under the arms, secured by a manually slid friction buckle. It is a hoist sling, not flotation.
- **Over-the-head foam buoyancy aids**: need manual side adjustment.
- **Breakaway lanyards** (US 5,027,477; US 6,539,588): release at a set force but perform no fitting work.

None uses the wearer's departure from the stowage point to perform a force-metered cinch.

## Competitive Landscape & Strategic Edge
Suppliers such as VIKING, Survitec and Lalizas compete on compliance, pack volume and price, within the paradigm of "stowed garment plus briefing". Aviation-style under-seat vests inherit the same search-and-fasten sequence. Worn-from-boarding schemes solve donning but add heat, hygiene and turnaround burdens.

The blind spot is treating the jacket as a garment needing human dexterity, instead of as transit infrastructure. Strap-hangers and fuel-hose breakaway couplings show that always-visible overhead hardware and calibrated-force disconnects are cheap, mature and self-explanatory. Moving the sizing step from the passenger's fingers to a fixed anchor and a force threshold removes the steps where untrained users fail. The parts are commodity foam, webbing and buckles.

## Core Concept
**Architecture.** Each device has four parts.

1. **Buoyant front.** Two closed-cell foam chest lobes (PE or PVC-nitrile, about 33 kg/m³) total 10.5 L. Net buoyancy is ρgV − foam weight ≈ 1025 × 9.81 × 0.0105 − 3.4 ≈ 100 N. The lobes are joined by a fabric hinge and occupy a 55–60 cm front arc. Each carries a rigid high-visibility grab handle on its outer end.

2. **Self-supporting band.** A 50 mm polypropylene webbing band is fixed to the left lobe, passes behind the wearer and enters a one-way cam buckle with a roller bar and thumb-release tab on the right lobe. A 1.5 mm HDPE stay is sewn into the back span. Like an oversized cable tie, the hoop therefore holds an open oval of about 58 × 36 cm (inner perimeter about 150 cm) until cinched. The surplus band leaves the buckle as a floating tail ending in a hand loop.

3. **Lanyard and coupling.** A 0.7 m slack lanyard runs from a fixed vessel anchor to the tail's hand loop. It ends in a reusable calibrated breakaway coupling (ball-detent or magnetic) with release force F_r = 70 ± 10 N.

4. **Stowage cradle.** Four overhead spring clips hold the hoop horizontal at 1.9–2.0 m above each seat, or on the seat-back top rail or canopy frame of open boats. Total clip retention is 30–40 N.

**Donning kinematics.** The only action is "grab both handles, pull down over your head". Hands grip the rim from outside, so the hoop passes head and shoulders and comes to rest under the armpits with both arms outside and above it. This is the motion of lowering a swim ring. There is no inside-out or back-to-front state: the handles are only on the foam side, and the foam ends up on the chest.

**Force-metered cinch.** The 0.7 m of slack lets the hoop clear head and shoulders before any draw begins. The cinch then runs in four steps.

1. Further movement in any direction (stepping to the aisle, sitting, falling) tensions the lanyard.
2. The tail draws through the buckle and the perimeter shrinks by up to 80 cm, from 150 cm to 70 cm.
3. Draw resistance stays low (webbing friction, estimated under 10 N) until the band is snug on the torso. Tension then rises steeply.
4. When lanyard force reaches F_r, the coupling separates and the cam holds the band.

Band tension at release is T ≈ F_r · e^(−μθ), where μ is the effective friction at the buckle bar and θ ≈ π is the wrap angle. The design target is T = 30–45 N, set by choosing F_r after measuring the buckle's μ. On a chest of radius about 0.15 m this gives contact pressure p = T/(w·r) ≈ 40/(0.05 × 0.15) ≈ 5 kPa, comparable to a firmly hand-tightened belt. A 25 mm elastic gusset in the band allows for breathing. The lanyard stays on the vessel. The tail (up to 0.8 m, buoyant) stays on the wearer as a buddy and retrieval line.

**Fallbacks.** If the wearer cannot move away, pulling the hand loop does the same cinch. If the device is never cinched, the band still stops at the armpits. That is degraded but functional. In an inverted flooded cabin, 100 N of buoyancy exceeds both the clip retention (≤40 N) and F_r. Undonned hoops therefore release themselves and float free as grab floats without any hydrostatic release unit. The thumb tab reopens a hoop that has cinched empty.

**Variants.**
- A child unit (girth 50–75 cm, reduced buoyancy, distinct colour) is mounted lower at family rows.
- An optional hinged foam chin flap on the lobes floats up for airway freeboard.
- The riskier optional embodiment replaces wearer motion with a ceiling spring balancer (constant-force retractor, about 50 N) that performs the draw. It needs a latch that prevents cinching before the hoop clears the shoulders. It is disclosed but not preferred, because of that timing risk.

**Maintenance.** The coupling is reusable. Crew can cycle every unit in drills and re-seat it in seconds. A coloured witness tab shows any hoop not at full open perimeter.

## Physics & Logical Feasibility
- **Buoyancy:** follows directly from foam volume. 100 N corresponds to ISO 12402-4 level-100 magnitude.
- **Cinch energy:** at most about 70 N × 0.8 m ≈ 56 J, trivially supplied by one step of body motion. Release force is roughly a tenth of adult body weight, so the wearer cannot be trapped by the lanyard.
- **Retention loads:** clip retention of 30–40 N holds a hoop of about 0.9 kg up to roughly 3 g of vertical acceleration (27 N), yet stays below the submerged buoyancy.
- **Band strength:** 50 mm polypropylene webbing has breaking strength in the kilonewton range, far above the load.
- **Sequencing:** 0.7 m of slack exceeds the travel from stowed height to a standing adult's armpit (about 0.6 m), so the cinch cannot begin before shoulder clearance.

Critical assumptions are that draw friction before snugness stays well below F_r, that an under-arm band with front buoyancy gives acceptable ride-up resistance and flotation attitude, and that garments do not jam the buckle. All three need testing.

## Implementation Readiness & Key Constraints
All parts are catalogue items: foam, webbing, HDPE stay, cam buckle, detent coupling and spring clips. A cost estimate that stays under the $500,000 ceiling:

| Step | Timing | Estimated cost |
|---|---|---|
| CAD and 30 prototypes; bench calibration of F_r against T | Months 0–4 | $90k |
| Dry-land donning trials with naive adults across the girth range, against a locker-jacket control | Months 4–8 | $70k |
| Pool trials for ride-up, freeboard, arms-raised slip-out and tail snagging, using ISO 12402-9 methods | Months 8–14 | $120k |
| 200-seat pilot on one domestic ferry, with vibration, UV and vandalism monitoring | Months 14–24 | $120k |
| Regulatory documentation and reserve | Throughout | $80k |

Key constraints are headroom and head-strike clearance, tamper resistance, and flag-state acceptance. Until equivalence is granted, the device would be carried in addition to mandated lifejackets.

## Utility & Purpose
The device collapses locate, size, orient, don and fasten into one gross-motor action on an object already in the passenger's line of sight. It is built for the 30–60 s window of a fast capsize. Because the hardware is its own signage and has only one usable configuration, the verbal briefing can shrink to a pictogram on the hoop where regulators permit. That would recover briefing time across the roughly 2 billion annual trips cited in the problem statement. Beneficiaries are passengers on domestic ferries, water buses, river taxis and tour boats. The benefit is largest for overloaded short-crossing services where trained crew cannot assist everyone.

## Potential Applications & Impact
Direct applications are retrofits to enclosed ferry cabins, open-deck benches, water taxis, floating restaurants and pontoon terminals. The under-arm geometry also suits quayside public rescue stations. A hoop on a post, with the lanyard anchored to it, would let a bystander don it in seconds before a wading rescue.

For research, the force-metered cinch is a general principle that turns the departure motion itself into calibrated work. It could carry over to evacuation harnesses, rapid-don respirator straps and child restraints, where untrained users must reach a fit defined by tension. If trials succeed, the device also gives regulators a measurable alternative to the 75%-in-one-minute benchmark. That could move standards from "donnable with instruction" toward "cannot be donned wrongly".

## Market/Adoption Feasibility (If Applicable)
Estimated unit bill of materials is $15–25 at volume, comparable to a foam SOLAS jacket, plus about $5 for the cradle and anchor. Installation uses existing overhead rails or seat frames with hand tools. Reusable couplings avoid consumables. Barriers are type approval, operator reluctance to carry duplicate equipment during the equivalence period, and cabin aesthetics and headroom. Enablers are domestic non-SOLAS fleets governed by national administrations, insurer and donor interest in ferry safety in high-fatality regions, and open publication, which lets any local workshop produce the device from commodity materials.

## Risk & Counterarguments
- **Slip-out and flotation attitude:** an under-arm band can slip if the arms are raised. A front-only 100 N device may not turn an unconscious wearer face-up. It may therefore fall short of LSA Code turning and freeboard requirements.
- **Premature release:** a snag-induced force spike could release the coupling early and leave slack.
- **Tail entanglement:** the retained tail could catch in wreckage.
- **Obstruction:** overhead hoops could obstruct or be vandalised, and floating hoops could clutter escape routes.
- **Body sizes outside range:** very large or small bodies, bulky clothing and wheelchair users need dedicated variants.
- **Regulation:** authorities may still mandate briefings, which would remove the time saving.

No trial data exist yet. All performance figures are calculations or estimates.

## Open Questions & Future Research Directions
- What is the best F_r and buckle friction across clothing types and girths?
- What is the measured donning time and error rate against conventional jackets in naive subjects?
- How much do ride-up and airway freeboard improve with and without the chin flap?
- Should a second low-force breakaway be added to the tail?
- How do crowds behave when hundreds of lanyards deploy at once?
- What is the durability under UV, salt and vibration?
- Would a hybrid with a small inflatable collar achieve turning performance?
- How should cradles be laid out for standing passengers and low ceilings?
- What is the regulatory pathway for equivalence under national domestic-vessel rules and ISO 12402?

## Novelty Threshold Justification
**Medium Novelty.** The elements are known individually: over-head donning, under-arm slings, cam buckles and breakaway lanyards. The new central mechanism is a slack-delayed vessel-anchored lanyard that performs the girth cinch as the wearer departs, and that ends the cinch at a calibrated force through a breakaway coupling. It is combined with a self-supporting open hoop on overhead display.

The closest art uses hand-pulled drawstraps ([US 6,394,866](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6394866), [US 5,702,279](https://patentimages.storage.googleapis.com/d0/1f/eb/22d7b7817f6780/US5702279.pdf)) or a manual friction buckle ([rescue strop](https://lifesavingsystems.com/store/lifesaving-systems-catalog.pdf)). Three searches found no anticipating disclosure. That is a limited search and does not prove absence.
